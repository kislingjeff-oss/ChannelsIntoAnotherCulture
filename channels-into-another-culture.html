1 · The channel

What is a channel?

A relationship strong enough to carry knowledge across a boundary is what I call a channel — and without knowing the word, I have been building channels all my life.Jeff Kisling, Knowledge withheld versus missing, July 14, 2026

Jeff Kisling grew up in a rural Quaker community in Iowa and went to Scattergood Friends School, a working farm school. Only late in life did he learn that two miles from his school lies the unmarked grave of Grace Yellowhair, a fifteen-year-old Arapaho girl who died at a Quaker industrial school for Native children in 1883. Her story was in Friends' own records the whole time. It never reached him.

Nobody hid it from him, at least not in his lifetime. No teacher refused to tell him. The knowledge simply had no way to travel from the people who carried it to the people who were formed by those schools. That missing route is what this whole guide is about, and Jeff gave it a name: a channel.

Four parts of one sentence

A relationshipNot a book, a website, a documentary or an app. A channel is made of people who know each other.
Strong enoughThere is a threshold. Being in the same room or following the same accounts doesn't clear it. Something has to be built, and building costs time and effort.
To carry knowledgeA channel isn't friendship for its own sake. Something travels along it: a story, an understanding, a way of seeing.
Across a boundaryBetween cultures, communities or generations. "If there is no boundary, you do not need a channel — you need a library."

Why "channel"? The water image

Knowledge can be thought of as water flowing through channels. Water doesn't reach where there are no channels — not because the water isn't there, but because the channel hasn't been built.Jeff Kisling, How Knowledge Travels, June 27, 2026

The image does three things at once. It separates existence from availability: the knowledge never went missing, it was carried all along, often at great cost. It moves the failure from persons to infrastructure, so the problem can be fixed without first deciding who to blame. And it names the remedy: you don't summon water, you build channels.

The image also breaks in an important place. An irrigation ditch runs one way, downhill, and isn't changed by what flows through it. A channel between people runs both ways and changes both ends. Knowledge flows toward you; labor, support, respect and your own knowledge flow back. In Jeff's words, "a one-way channel is extraction."

How a channel is built

Understanding of that kind arrives only through a channel: a relationship with the people who hold that knowledge, built over years, entered by invitation, and carrying in both directions.Jeff Kisling, Defining statement revised, August 25, 2026
Built over yearsTwo years, three, nine. Not a weekend trip or a single event. "There is no fast channel."
Entered by invitationYou go where you're asked. What you control is whether you keep showing up, not the terms.
Carrying both directionsWhat you receive comes with what you owe: work, support, credit given back, and a life that changes.

Why this matters for you

Almost every young adult lives near a culture whose history, language, food, faith and grief they couldn't describe. You may have read about it or seen it on a screen. But the understanding that would let you respond well hasn't arrived, and it can't arrive through reading alone. It arrives through relationship.

The hardest part is that you can't see the problem from where you stand. You can't go looking for something you don't know you're missing. Jeff calls this the Gap. Something has to break through first: a story, an invitation, an encounter. If something has already broken through for you, your first responsibility is to become that breakthrough for someone else.

02 · What the Panthers knew

Power built by meeting needs, and coalitions where nobody had to disappear

Most people remember the Black Panther Party through berets and raised fists. Those images are real. But much of what the Panthers did, day after day, was feed children, run clinics and teach. Huey P. Newton called it survival pending revolution: don't wait for the system to change before meeting people's needs.

October 1966 · Oakland

The Party is founded

Huey P. Newton and Bobby Seale write the Ten-Point Program. Point one: "We want freedom. We want power to determine the destiny of our Black Community."

January 1969 · Oakland

Free Breakfast for Children

Begins at St. Augustine's Episcopal Church and spreads to cities across the country, feeding thousands of children each morning. Clinics, sickle cell screening and liberation schools follow.

1969 · Chicago

The Rainbow Coalition

Fred Hampton, twenty years old, links the Panthers with the Young Lords, a Puerto Rican youth group, and the Young Patriots, poor white Southern migrants. Each group keeps its own leaders and its own neighborhood programs. Hampton is killed in a police raid coordinated with the FBI that December. He was twenty-one.

November 1970 · Boston College

Intercommunalism

In a speech at Boston College, Newton argues that nations are no longer the real containers of power. Communities are now the real units of struggle and care.

The lesson for crossing cultures

Autonomy and coalition can coexist. You don't have to become someone else to build a real relationship with them. Trust is built through shared work: the breakfast program, not the rally. And as SNCC organizers told white volunteers in the 1960s, organize where you are too. Open your own community's records and arrive at someone else's door prepared.

03 · Revolutionary Intercommunal Mutualism

From terminal to node

Revolutionary Intercommunal Mutualism (RIM) is a present-day framework that turns Newton's idea into daily practice. Its roots are the Black radical tradition and Indigenous lifeways. Its central image is a choice between two ways a community can be connected to the world.

Terminal

Plugged into systems it doesn't control, "receiving inputs, transmitting outputs, according to protocols it didn't write."

Node

A community with organized capacity to make and act on its own decisions, linked to others side by side.

RIM tests a community with five honest questions. Read them and notice what every one of them is really asking.

  1. If the grid shut down for thirty days, where would your food come from, and who would you feed besides yourself?
  2. If someone needed care but couldn't get into a clinic, who would you call?
  3. If your block lost power in extreme heat or cold, where would people gather, and who knows how to keep them alive?
  4. If communication went down, how would you reach your people?
  5. If harm happened, who could respond without calling police?

Every question is about who. Material strength runs on people who know each other well enough to act together under pressure. Where those relationships cross a boundary of culture, they are channels. That's why the two frameworks fit together:

04 · The map of cultural knowledge

Sixteen major bodies of cultural knowledge

"Learning about a culture" usually means something vague. But a culture is many bodies of knowledge, each with its own keepers and its own rules about who may receive it. Every body has layers, from what's offered to anyone to what isn't yours to take at all.

Open doorOffered publicly by the community itself. Just show up.
Shared in relationshipTaught when you're present and trusted, over time.
Earned over yearsTaught to people who've made lasting commitments.
Held by the communityNot for outsiders. Respecting that is the knowledge.
Filter by what's open to you
If you are told that something is not for you, you have not failed to learn. You have learned what that culture protects, and that it trusts you enough to tell you so.From the field guide, chapter 8
05 · The ladder of invitation

Where are you standing?

"Invitation precedes entry" can sound like a reason to wait at home until someone calls. It isn't. Invitation is a ladder of four rungs, and the first one is almost always open already. Tap a rung.

When white people would ask me how to support communities suffering injustice, I would tell them to just show up at events organized by these communities. … By JUST attend I mean don't do anything else until you are invited to do so.Jeff Kisling, Working with different communities or cultures, May 2, 2026

07 · A year of building

You can't schedule a channel. You can schedule the conditions.

1 · Ground

Open your own family's, school's or town's records. Learn whose land you live on. Find three open doors and go.

Ask nothing of anyone yet.
2 · Prepare

Read the community's own writers. Learn greetings and protocol from a community source. Form a group of three.

Ask "What should I know before I come?"
3 · Accompany

Answer a posted call for work. Show up on schedule. Do only the work that was posted.

Write down every correction, and what you changed.
4 · Review

Ask, on their terms, what helped and what should stop. Renew or end your commitment. Bring one friend to rung one.

Has anything in your life actually changed?

A sixth honest question

If the community you support called tomorrow, would they call you?

Would they call someone who already knows them well enough to hear the request, understand it, and act on it without needing it explained? If yes, you have a channel. If "maybe, eventually," you're building one. If no, the open door is where to start, and it's open today.

This page is not a channel either. It's directions to one. Close the laptop and go find your open door.
